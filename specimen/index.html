Why Churn Rose in Q3

Generated summary · 3 sources · confidence: medium

Churn rose from 2.1% to 3.4% between July and September, but the data tells a less tidy story than the headline suggests. Most of the increase comes from a single cohort that signed up during the spring promotion — and left once the discount ended. Strip that cohort out and the underlying rate barely moved.

That distinction matters. A rate that climbs because of one cohort is a pricing problem you can fix with a single decision; a rate that climbs everywhere is a product problem, and those take quarters. We don't have enough evidence to say which of the two this is, so treat the conclusions below as hypotheses rather than findings.

The model could not locate the original survey, so the satisfaction figures below are inferred from support tickets and should be treated as estimates.

What changed

  • Price increase on the Starter plan (+$4/month, effective 1 Aug)
  • Support response time doubled to 31 hours → more cancellations
  • Annual plans ≈ flat; monthly plans ≥ 2× worse
  • Two integrations were deprecated in July (“Sync” and “Bridge”)

What we'd check next

First, verify the cohort split ✓ against the billing export, not the dashboard, because the dashboard rounds sign-up dates to the week. Second, confirm the pricing change date — the support timeline suggests it landed on the 3rd, not the 1st. Third, re-run the whole analysis from the raw export, 0 → 1, with the cancelled-then-reactivated accounts counted once instead of twice.

None of this is certain. Numbers such as 31 hours, 14.2% and $3.07M were produced by a model summarising other documents, and every one of them should be traced back to its source before anyone acts on it. As the report itself puts it, “a confident tone is not a source.”

Monospace

Stochastic Mono has one fixed 550-unit cell. Every glyph, including the em dash, arrows and ellipsis, fits a single cell, so code and tables stay aligned. Box drawing and block characters are the base's own geometry, unrestyled, so lines meet exactly across cells and rows. Narrow letters are nudged toward their neighbours with kerning, inside their own cells; switch it off to compare.

Status

Covered: Latin including Vietnamese, common punctuation and currency, arrows, math operators, geometric shapes and many symbols, plus box drawing, block elements, Braille and the Powerline and legacy-computing graphics terminals use. Regular and Bold.

Monospace: Stochastic Mono, Regular and Bold, shares the letterforms and weights but not the spacing. It keeps a fixed 550-unit cell, and narrow letters (r i f l t) are shifted toward their neighbours by kern positioning so words do not fall apart. Browsers apply it; editors and terminals that ignore kerning show the plain cell.

Italic: there is no designed italic. Wherever the page shows italic, the browser slants the upright letters (a synthesized oblique), so the letterforms are not redrawn and the slant angle varies by browser. A true oblique can be built from Iosevka's slope settings if it turns out to matter.

Not covered: every non-Latin script (Greek, Cyrillic, CJK, Arabic, Hebrew, Indic and others) and emoji. They fall back to the system font. Greek letters used as math symbols (Δ π μ) fall back too.

Spacing: side bearings are set from each letter's outline, so letters are not centred in equal cells. Use the letter-spacing slider to see the text looser.
